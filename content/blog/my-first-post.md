After working as a Senior ML Engineer at CVS Health, I’m now pursuing a PhD at **Monash University**, exploring **AI safety in legal reasoning and decision-making**.

The question that interests me is how AI systems behave as we train, adapt and improve them. How can we tell whether an apparent improvement makes a system more dependable? And how can we identify changes that move its behaviour away from what we intended?

## How My Research Interests Have Evolved

My initial interests centred on graph neural networks and neuro-symbolic approaches to legal reasoning. As I’ve explored the literature and discussed possible directions with my supervisors, my focus has shifted towards the safety and alignment of AI systems used in legal settings.

I’m still developing the specific research question. At this stage, I’m concentrating on understanding the evidence, identifying meaningful problems and designing small experiments to investigate them.

## Questions I’m Exploring

**How do training and feedback shape model behaviour?**

I’m interested in how adapting a model for a particular task affects its behaviour elsewhere. In legal applications, this includes how a system responds to different professional roles, user expectations and feedback.

**What distinguishes an error from an alignment problem?**

Bias, sycophancy, hallucination and deception raise different questions about what a system is doing and why. I want to understand where these concepts overlap, how researchers measure them, and what evidence supports each interpretation.

**Does iterative improvement produce better decisions?**

One question I’m exploring is whether repeated feedback improves the substance of a legal assessment or mainly makes it score better with an evaluator. This involves examining the evidence behind an answer alongside the scores it receives.

Recursive self-improvement is a longer-term interest: systems that participate in modifying their own improvement process. I want to investigate what this could mean for maintaining intended behaviour as a system changes.

## Why Legal AI?

Legal decision-making provides a setting where evidence, applicable rules, competing interpretations and accountability all matter. My current interests include reasoning about real Malaysian contract disputes and the responsibilities involved when AI supports legal professionals.

I’m also interested in questions surrounding AI in judicial roles: which tasks might be appropriate, how uncertainty should be handled, and where human judgment and responsibility remain essential.

## What Comes Next?

My immediate work involves reading foundational and empirical AI-safety research, examining documented incidents relevant to legal practice, and conducting small, controlled experiments.

I’ll use this blog to share what I’m learning, explain the methods behind the papers I read, and reflect on how the research direction develops.
